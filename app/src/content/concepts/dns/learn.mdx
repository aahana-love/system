You don't remember your friends' phone numbers. You tap a name, and the phone looks up the number. DNS does the same job for the internet: you type a name, and DNS finds the number, the IP address, that computers actually use to deliver data.

## Why names need translating

Every server on the internet is reached by its IP address, something like `203.0.113.10`. Routers only understand these numbers. People are bad at remembering numbers, and the numbers change: when a company moves a service to new servers, the address changes but the name `amazon.in` stays the same.

So before your browser can open `www.amazon.in`, it has to ask one question: **what is the IP address for this name?** The system that answers is the Domain Name System.

## No single phone book

There are hundreds of millions of domain names, and they change every minute. One server holding all of them would be a single point of failure and would collapse under the whole world's lookups. DNS splits the work into a tree, and each level only knows who to ask next:

- **Root servers** know where the servers for each top-level domain (`.com`, `.in`, `.org`) are.
- **TLD servers** for `.in` know which nameservers are responsible for `amazon.in`.
- **Authoritative nameservers** for `amazon.in` hold the actual records, such as the address of `www.amazon.in`.

You never talk to these servers yourself. A **recursive resolver** does the running around for you. It's usually run by your ISP, or it's a public one like Google's `8.8.8.8` or Cloudflare's `1.1.1.1`.

## What you just saw

1. Your browser checks its own cache, then asks the operating system, which has a cache too.
2. On a miss, the OS sends one question to the recursive resolver: "give me the final answer for `www.amazon.in`."
3. The resolver asks a root server, which replies with a **referral**: "I don't know, but here are the `.in` servers."
4. The `.in` servers refer it to `amazon.in`'s own nameservers.
5. The authoritative nameserver answers with the address and a TTL (time to live).
6. Each layer on the way back keeps a copy until that TTL runs out.

The first lookup took four network round trips. The repeat cost nothing, because the answer was already sitting in your browser. Most real lookups are answered from a cache somewhere along the way. That's how DNS handles the whole world's traffic without the root servers melting.

<Callout kind="interview" title="DNS in one sentence">
DNS is a distributed, cached, hierarchical lookup: root → TLD → authoritative nameserver, with a recursive resolver doing the walking and TTLs deciding how long each answer is reused.
</Callout>
